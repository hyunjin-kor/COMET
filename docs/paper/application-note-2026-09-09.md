# COMET: Catalyst Overall Manufacturing Estimation Tool

Authors, affiliations and corresponding-author contact: [to be supplied by the authors].

## Abstract

Early-stage catalyst cost estimates depend on operating conditions that synthesis reports seldom state and on metal prices that change, yet estimates rarely document either. COMET is desktop software that implements the Step Method, a published method that prices a synthesis as a sequence of industrial processing steps, and adds source-linked preparation records, batch costing from stated operating conditions, metal price histories, and ranking sensitivity tests. It reproduces the method's three published examples line by line; the largest price difference, 6.65<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[1].residual_pct -->%, arises from the margin rule. Production scale changes the price of Ni/Al₂O₃ 9.1-fold, metal loading controls that of Ru/Al₂O₃, and recovered mass and treatment time control laboratory batch cost. Over 92<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.months --> months of metal prices, the lowest-cost candidate changes in 6<!-- price-crossovers-2026-09-21/price_crossovers.json:summary.monthly.cost_winner --> of 30<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.families --> reaction families. COMET estimates cost, not performance; coupling with performance-prediction models is planned.

Keywords: catalyst manufacturing cost; preparation procedures; sensitivity analysis; metal prices; software.

## Introduction

Catalyst manufacturing cost affects process economics alongside activity, selectivity, and stability. In biomass-conversion design reports, it amounted to 3–9% of installed equipment cost and shifted the minimum fuel selling price by up to ±10%, yet researchers outside catalyst companies have few means of estimating it.<sup>1</sup>

Two published tools address this need. The Step Method estimates the purchase price of a precommercial catalyst by mapping its laboratory synthesis onto industrial processing steps with hourly costs and adding materials, overheads, and a scale-dependent margin; for three commercial catalysts, its estimates fell within ±20% of market prices.<sup>1</sup> CatCost built the method into spreadsheet and web tools and added dedicated-plant costing, spent-catalyst value, sensitivity analysis, and a weighted cost and life-cycle comparison of candidates.<sup>2</sup> Other studies costed individual synthesis routes<sup>3,4</sup> or propagated parameter uncertainty through techno-economic models.<sup>5</sup>

These tools take conditions and prices as given, which leaves three problems when candidates come from the literature. First, the operating conditions behind processing cost are seldom reported, and an estimate that fills such gaps unstated cannot be checked. Second, metal prices move, so a cost order from one date need not hold on another. Third, when cost joins other criteria, min–max normalized scores depend on the candidates included, so removing one can reorder the others.<sup>6,7</sup>

COMET records these conditions with every estimate: it implements the Step Method and adds source-linked preparation records and batch costing from stated conditions (first problem), metal price histories (second), and ranking sensitivity tests (third). We ask whether COMET reproduces the published method, which variables set the estimated cost at laboratory and production scale, and how often observed metal prices change the cost order and the recommended candidate.

## Implementation

### Software design

COMET is a desktop application (Figure 1): a formulation, preparation method, production scale, and price basis give an estimate listing its data sources, assumptions, and missing conditions; saved estimates can be repriced, compared, and ranked. A Python backend (FastAPI, SQLite) serves a React/TypeScript interface in English and Korean, packaged for Windows with Electron.

### Cost models

COMET has two cost models. Production-scale estimates follow the Step Method (Figure 2a).<sup>1</sup> Materials cost is Cₘ = Σ<sub>i</sub>w<sub>i</sub>c<sub>i</sub>, with mass fraction w<sub>i</sub> and price c<sub>i</sub> of component i. Processing cost is Cₚ = 24TIH/M, where H is the summed hourly cost of the selected processing steps (2017 basis), I a producer-price-index factor, T the campaign duration in days, and M the production scale (kg of catalyst per order), which sets equipment size, the throughput of each step, and T. The selling price is P = (Cₘ + Cₚ)(1 + g)(1 + s)/(1 − m), with general and administrative (G&A) fraction g, sales, administrative, research, and distribution (SARD) fraction s, and margin m from the published scale correlation. P is the price a buyer would pay; cost below means P per kilogram. COMET provides 28<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:manufacturing.20.template_count --> modifiable preparation methods and identifies substituted or uncosted steps.

Laboratory batch costing replaces these terms with the batch's reagent purchases and operating conditions: electricity, equipment time, operator labor, gases, and transfers between operations (eqs S1–S6). The sum is divided by the recovered dry mass before overheads and margin are applied, and a missing required condition stops the calculation instead of assuming a value.

### Library and preparation records

The library holds 116<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.candidates --> formulations in 30<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.families --> reaction families (23 of powder catalysts, 7 of electrocatalysts), each with a composition, preparation method, production scale, and route and performance scores. Compositions follow a literature formulation for 83<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:screening_basis_counts.literature_architecture_proxy --> candidates, an engineering baseline for 29<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:screening_basis_counts.engineering_proxy -->, and vendor or market data for 4; all are screening models, not verified recipes, and their estimates add assumed route allowances (Figure 2c). Each candidate is priced at its library loading, from the cited source where reported and otherwise assumed, not at a common loading (Table S7). Preparation records link candidates to what their sources report: a review verified 401<!-- manufacturing-2026-09-14/review_summary.json:crossref_dois --> digital object identifiers (DOIs) with Crossref and transcribed 92<!-- manufacturing-2026-09-14/review_summary.json:profiles --> sample-specific records from 71<!-- manufacturing-2026-09-14/review_summary.json:primary_sources --> primary sources for 75<!-- manufacturing-2026-09-14/review_summary.json:candidates_with_profile --> candidates, noting composition or source discrepancies in 26<!-- manufacturing-2026-09-14/review_summary.json:source_mismatches -->. Each value carries its DOI and source section, unreported conditions stay blank, and a record documents a source without validating the library formulation (Section S6).

### Prices and ranking

The reference basis stores monthly averages from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) Primary Commodity Price System<sup>10</sup> and Johnson Matthey<sup>8</sup> for metals and U.S. import unit values for supports,<sup>11</sup> each with source, date, and retrieval time (Section S5). Results use the August 2026<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:basis_month --> reference basis; the same series give 92<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.months --> monthly price states from January 2019<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.first_month -->. A second basis uses current quotations such as Johnson Matthey's<sup>8</sup> and Westmetall's.<sup>9</sup> A price-reliability score averages source grades by share of materials cost; it rates source quality, not estimate accuracy.

Within a family, candidates are ranked by a weighted sum of four 0–100 scores: cost, price reliability, route, and performance. The cost score is min–max normalized from 100 (least expensive) to 0 (most expensive); route and performance scores are assigned from the literature. The balanced weighting used below gives cost the largest weight (0.40–0.45).

## Results and Discussion

### Verification

The source of the Step Method tabulates three demonstration cases (Pt/C, Ni/Al₂O₃, and a fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) catalyst) with every intermediate line and a market price,<sup>1</sup> and their data were entered as published. For Pt/C and Ni/Al₂O₃, COMET reproduces every line before the margin within 0.4%, the rounding of the published table (Table S1), and the Pt/C price matches (60.34<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[0].comet_usd_per_lb --> USD/kg, platinum value excluded). The Ni/Al₂O₃ price is 42.37<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[1].comet_usd_per_lb --> against 45.39<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[1].published_usd_per_lb --> USD/kg (−6.65<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[1].residual_pct -->%), and the whole difference lies in the margin: the table applies 33<!-- submission-2026-09-21/table62_reproduction_2026-09-21.json:[1].margin.table_footnote_pct_of_premargin -->% of pre-margin cost, whereas the scale correlation of the same source, which COMET applies, gives 24<!-- submission-2026-09-21/table62_reproduction_2026-09-21.json:[1].margin.comet_pct_of_premargin -->%. For FCC, costed at the effective production rate the source states (60,781.4<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[2].effective_rate_ton_per_day --> kg/day), COMET gives 5.37<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[2].comet_usd_per_lb --> against 5.31<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[2].published_usd_per_lb --> USD/kg (+1.16<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[2].residual_pct -->%). Against the market prices of the source, the estimates deviate by −19.7<!-- submission-2026-09-21/table62_reproduction_2026-09-21.json:[0].market.comet_vs_market_pct -->%, −9.9<!-- submission-2026-09-21/table62_reproduction_2026-09-21.json:[1].market.comet_vs_market_pct -->%, and −10.7% (Figure 2b), within the ±20% reported for the method. These results verify the calculation, not new estimates: the market comparison covers three catalysts at mid-2017 prices.

### Production-scale cost structure

Figure 2c divides the selling price of the lowest-cost candidate in each of the 23 powder-catalyst families. Materials exceed half of the price in only 3 families, processing exceeds materials in 12, and G&A, SARD, margin, and route allowances take 24–42%. Most estimates thus depend on processing assumptions as much as on materials prices; the Step Method standardizes them, whereas a laboratory preparation must supply its own.

### Production-scale parametric analyses

For industrial production, the Step Method derives operating conditions from the preparation method and production scale, so the user chooses composition, method, and scale; each was varied in turn for two alumina-supported catalysts prepared by incipient wetness impregnation (Figure 4a–c; Table S8). ### Preparation-method crossovers

The preparation method can also reverse a cost order. Step Method processing cost depends on the operation sequence and the scale, not on the composition, so at 18,143.7<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.order_size_tons --> kg the five methods shift every supported-metal candidate by up to 1.26 USD/kg. Where materials costs differ by less, the method decides: Ni/γ-Al₂O₃ costs 0.86<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.ammonia_cracking_pair.materials_gap_per_lb --> USD/kg less in materials than Co/MgO–La₂O₃, but by deposition–precipitation it costs 0.09 USD/kg more than the cobalt catalyst by incipient wetness (Figure 4f). Priced under each method, the 70<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.summary.candidates --> supported-metal candidates of 20<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.summary.families --> families change the lowest-cost candidate in 5<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.summary.lowest_cost_depends_on_template --> (Table S9). Equal activity across methods is not assumed.

### Recommendation sensitivity

A change in cost order need not change the recommendation. In ammonia cracking, the first-ranked candidate under balanced weighting remains Co/MgO–La₂O₃ in all 92<!-- price-crossovers-2026-09-21/price_crossovers.json:families[1].periods.monthly.summary.winner_counts.app_winner.co-mgo-la2o3 --> months: the cost score is normalized within the family, and Ru/MgO, at 2,925.80<!-- methods-2026-09-21/methods_study.json:normalization.example.removed_cost --> USD/kg, fixes the expensive end, so the two least expensive candidates keep cost scores of at least 99.7 and the other scores decide their order. Removing Ru/MgO contracts the scale, magnifies the 1.17 USD/kg difference between them, and reverses their ranking, a known property of min–max normalization<sup>6,7</sup> that retaining the original range prevents. Over all families, removing one lower-ranked candidate changes the first-ranked candidate in 8<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.candidate_removal_winner_changes --> of 86<!-- robustness-2026-09-21/decision_robustness.json:summary.candidate_removal_cases --> tests (Figure S6). Current quotations collected on September 21, 2026<!-- submission-2026-09-21/live_basis_2026-09-21.json:observation_started_at_utc --> change the first-ranked candidate in 4<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:live_reference_comparison.changed_by_profile.balanced --> of 30<!-- price-crossovers-2026-09-21/price_crossovers.json:summary.monthly.families --> families, partly because cobalt, without a current quotation, falls back to an annual price of lower reliability grade (Table S10). A recommendation should therefore carry its candidate set, price basis, and date.

### Limitations

COMET estimates manufacturing cost only, not activity, selectivity, or lifetime, so a more expensive catalyst can still be more economical per unit of product;<sup>12</sup> route and performance scores are literature-based screening judgments, not measured or predicted activity. Accuracy against industrial prices is not established, because no public price matched a library formulation in grade, scale, date, and delivery boundary (Table S12).

## Conclusions

COMET implements the Step Method, documents with each estimate its data sources, price date, and candidate set, and reproduces the three published examples line by line; the largest price difference, 6.65<!-- submission-2026-09-21/paper_summary_2026-09-21.json:table62[1].residual_pct -->%, arises from the margin rule. Production scale sets the estimated cost of a base-metal catalyst, metal loading that of a precious-metal catalyst, and treatment time and recovered mass that of a laboratory batch, which only 1 of 92<!-- manufacturing-2026-09-14/review_summary.json:profiles --> preparation records reports. Observed metal prices changed the lowest-cost candidate in 6<!-- price-crossovers-2026-09-21/price_crossovers.json:summary.monthly.cost_winner --> of 30<!-- price-crossovers-2026-09-21/price_crossovers.json:summary.monthly.families --> families, the preparation method could change it in 5<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.summary.lowest_cost_depends_on_template --> of 20<!-- whatif-2026-09-21/whatif_study.json:route_assignment.summary.families -->, and recommendations changed with the candidate set and the price basis. Cost comparisons should therefore state composition, preparation method, production scale, assumed operating conditions, price basis and date, and candidate set. Because COMET does not evaluate performance, we plan to couple it with performance-prediction models, to compare cost per unit of product, and to validate it against industrial prices under matched conditions.
